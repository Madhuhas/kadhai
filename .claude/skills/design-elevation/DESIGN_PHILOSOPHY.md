# Design philosophy

The judgement behind the process. Read this when a decision feels arbitrary, or when the result is technically correct and still not good.

## Expertise shows in the details, not the effects

Amateur work announces itself with gradients, shadows and animation. Expert work is recognised by things most people cannot name: the spacing is consistent, the numbers line up, the hover state exists, the heading does not end on a lonely word. Spend effort where nobody will consciously notice it. That is what reads as quality.

## Bold in one place, quiet everywhere else

A design needs a point of view, and a point of view needs a single strong decision: an oversized number, an unusual split, one saturated colour. Make that choice boldly. Then make everything else calm enough to let it land. Two bold ideas compete; five is noise.

**Be bold when**
- the piece has one clear message or number,
- it will be seen briefly and must be remembered,
- the subject has a character worth expressing.

**Be subtle when**
- the piece is a tool used for hours,
- the content is dense or changes constantly,
- the user's own content is the star.

## Unique does not mean busy

Being specific to a subject comes from choosing well, not from adding more. A kitchen-themed product is not made distinctive by a pattern of forks; it is made distinctive by a warm palette, language that fits, and one detail that could only belong there. If uniqueness is being pursued by addition, stop and subtract instead.

## Restraint is a skill

Every element costs attention. The reader pays that cost whether or not the element helps. So the default answer to "should I add this?" is no, and the question for everything already present is "what happens if it goes?" Whitespace is not empty; it is what makes the rest legible.

## Systems over instances

One good-looking card is easy. A design is the rule that makes the hundredth card look right without thought. Decide the scale, the palette roles and the component rules, then obey them. Consistency is what makes a thing feel designed rather than assembled. When you break the system, do it once, visibly, for a reason.

## Design should feel inevitable

The best result looks as though it could not have been otherwise: the layout follows from the content, the emphasis follows from what matters, the colour follows from meaning. If a choice needs explaining to be understood, it is probably decoration. Aim for the reaction "of course" rather than "wow".

## Function is the floor, never the ceiling

A beautiful thing that is hard to use has failed. Clarity, legibility, accessibility and speed come first and are not traded for style. But stopping at functional is stopping at the first draft. The work is to keep the function intact and add care.

## Respect the context

The design lives somewhere: in a product with its own identity, on a slow laptop, in a dark room, on a phone. Work with the existing system and constraints rather than against them. A modest improvement that fits is worth more than a striking one that does not belong.

## Know when to stop

Elevation has diminishing returns. When the hierarchy is clear, the system is consistent, the details are right and the removal test finds nothing more to take away, deliver it. Further fiddling usually makes things worse.
